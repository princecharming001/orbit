# Building a community and inviting people in

Sources: the Community Canvas; Orbit's own community goal design (docs/plan/16 §7.3); practitioner guides from Commsor and the Community Club. Checked 2026-10-31.

The shape of it. A community is a group of people who keep showing up for each other around something they share: a field, a school, a city, a problem, an identity. The founder's job early is to pick the people, host well, and make it easy to come back. Start smaller than feels right: eight to fifteen people who already like each other's company, a clear reason to gather, a regular rhythm (monthly is enough), and one person who hosts. Growth comes from members inviting people they vouch for, not from a launch.

The invitation. Specific and personal: a named event or group, when and where, who else is coming (two or three names they would recognise), and why this person in particular would fit or add something. "We're getting fifteen student founders together on the 12th at the library's upstairs room; Priya and Tom from the hackathon are coming and your work on X is exactly the kind of thing people want to hear about. Would you like to join us?" Under eighty words. Never a mass BCC, never "join our community" with no event behind it, never a Discord link as the whole message. One bump after a week, framed as a reminder with the detail again.

Speakers and guests. Someone who spoke on campus or wrote something the group read is invited with the specific thing they said and what the group would ask them about; the ask is thirty to forty five minutes, with the format, the audience size and the date in the message. Senior people say yes to a well run room of twenty engaged students far more often than to a coffee chat. Thank them the same day with one thing from the conversation and what the group took from it.

Hosting. Start on time, introduce people to each other by what they have in common, give the room one question to work on, end on time, and send a short note afterwards with what happened and when the next one is. Keep a list of members with how they came in and who brought them. Ask members what they want before planning the next three events. The quiet members matter: write to them individually.

Partners and sponsors. A department, a club, a company's university team or a local firm will often give a room, food or a speaker in exchange for being named; ask with the plan, the numbers and the dates in one message, early, and report back afterwards with photos and attendance. A sponsor is told what they get and what they do not (no mailing list, no pitch slot unless agreed).

Who to talk to for advice. People who run communities the student admires (club presidents, meetup organisers, community managers at companies), asked how they got their first twenty members to come back and what they would do differently. Fifteen minutes, with a specific question about a problem the student is having.

What never happens: a community announced before it has met twice, members added to a group chat without asking, the same generic invite to a hundred people, an event with no host, a sponsor promised access to members, or a guest invited without the date and the format. Register: friendly, concrete, generous, specific about who and when.
