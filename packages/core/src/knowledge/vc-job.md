# Getting a job in venture capital

Sources: John Gannon's VC job board and guides; the Kauffman Fellows and Venture University descriptions of entry paths; packages/core/src/drafts/register.ts (venture questions). Checked 2026-10-31. Fellowship and scout program dates change every year.

The shape of the market: venture firms are small. A typical seed or Series A fund has five to twenty investment staff and hires an analyst or associate every year or two, often without posting the role. There is no campus recruiting cycle; a handful of firms run analyst programs or summer internships with applications usually in the winter and spring, and some larger platforms run structured programs. [CHANGES YEARLY: search the web for a firm's current analyst program or fellowship before stating when it opens.] Most junior investors come from one of three routes: two years in banking or consulting, a few years operating at a startup (growth, product, engineering), or a student fellowship and scout network that turns into a role. Being known to the firm before a seat opens is how most seats get filled.

Entry roles: analyst (sourcing, research, memos, data), associate (sourcing plus diligence and portfolio support, often after banking or an MBA), and on the platform side community, talent or content roles that can lead to investing. Student programs: campus scout programs (a small cheque book or a referral bonus for finding founders at your school), fellowships that run a semester or a summer with a cohort, and university venture funds run by students. Sourcing is the junior job: finding founders early, keeping a list, writing crisp notes. A student with a sourcing track record (founders they know, a newsletter, a campus fund) is far more interesting than one with a finance resume and nothing else.

The process: an introduction or a cold note that lands because of a point of view, a few conversations with the team, often a take-home (source three companies in a space and write why, or diligence a company and write an investment memo), case questions on a startup's model and market, and a conversation with the partners. Firms want judgment, taste, hustle and writing. The interviews are about the student's view: a sector they know, a company they would back and why, a company the firm backed and what they would have asked.

Who to talk to: analysts and associates (how they got in, what their week actually looks like, what they own on a deal), principals and partners only with a point of view ready and usually through a warm route. Founders the firm backed are a good side door: they know the partners and how they decide.

What to ask: how they got into venture and what their week looks like; how they source and what a first meeting with a founder looks like; what junior people at their firm own on a deal; whether they would recommend operating first; how to prepare for venture recruiting now; what they look for in people early in their careers who want to invest. Never "do you have any openings" and never a pitch for the student's own startup in a note that asks about a job.

Register: short, direct, a view in the first two sentences. Email or LinkedIn both work; investors live in both. "Hi" and first name. Five sentences, one specific thing about a company they backed or a post they wrote, one ask of fifteen minutes. One bump after a week, then stop. A thank-you with one new idea or company in it is remembered.

Preparation: pick two sectors and know twenty companies in each, write short memos on three startups the student would back, follow the firm's recent investments and have a view on them, and build a habit of meeting founders on campus. Writing in public (a newsletter, a few posts with real analysis) is the most reliable signal a student can create.
