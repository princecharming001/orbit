# Software engineering recruiting

Sources: docs/plan/15-outreach-playbook.md §3 (tech); Levels.fyi and the firms' university programs pages for timing; the student tracking data in plan 15 §5. Checked 2026-10-31. Posting dates change every year and by company.

The timeline: big tech and most large companies post summer internships for the following year in August to October and hire on a rolling basis until the seats are gone, often by December; some open earlier (July) and some post into the spring. [CHANGES YEARLY: search the web for a company's current internship posting before stating when it opens.] New grad roles follow the same calendar. Startups hire year round, usually two to three months before they need someone, and many never post an internship until someone asks. Return offers from the summer fill a large share of new grad seats, so the internship is the goal. Timing beats polish: an application within a few days of a posting is read; one a month later often is not.

The process: an online application, often an online assessment (a timed coding test, a HackerRank or CodeSignal), one or two technical phone screens (data structures and algorithms, sometimes a practical coding exercise), then a virtual or onsite loop of three to five interviews: coding, sometimes system design for new grads, and a behavioural round. Some companies run a team match after the offer. Big tech interview on fundamentals; startups interview on what you have built and whether you can ship. Referrals move an application from the portal pile to a recruiter's queue at most large companies and are easy to give; nobody minds being asked by someone they have talked to once.

Who to talk to: a recently joined engineer or a peer individual contributor on a team you would join, an intern from last summer, an alum two or three years out. Not the VP and not a recruiter who does not own the posting. Messaging several people at one company individually is fine; never say so. Hiring managers are only written to cold with proof: one clickable artifact built on their product or in their domain.

What to ask: what their team owns and what the stack looks like day to day; how intern projects get picked and how much ships; what the strongest interns did differently in their first month; what to practise before interviews there. A senior engineer or manager is asked what they look for in interns and new grads, or for a pointer to someone on the team closer to the intern class. Proof beats adjectives: one deployed app, a repo or a PDF, zero sentences about passion.

Referrals: ask before applying through the portal (many applicant systems cannot attach a referral afterwards), with the two minute package: the exact role and requisition ID with a link, the resume, one line of fit, and an out. An alum, a mutual contact or someone who posted that their team is hiring can be asked directly; a stranger gets a question first.

Register: "Hi" and first name, contractions, five to seven sentences, a subject that says what the email is ("Backend intern application, built a rate limiter on your API"). Sign with first name only. One bump at five to six days, then stop for good. LinkedIn works well in tech; cold email to an engineer gets roughly one reply in ten, a referred application roughly one interview in four, a portal application one or two in a hundred.

Interview preparation: two to three months of daily practice on the common problem patterns (arrays and hashing, two pointers, trees, graphs, dynamic programming), talking out loud while solving, mock interviews with peers, and a one minute story for each project on the resume with a number in it. For startups, be ready to walk through something you built end to end and to say what you would do differently.

Offers: tech offers often come with one to three weeks and companies will usually extend once when another process is near a decision; competing offers can be negotiated for new grads at large companies, politely and with a number.
