# Academic and research collaboration

Sources: faculty advice on cold emailing professors (Matt Might, Philip Guo, the Cornell and MIT undergraduate research offices); packages/core/src/drafts/register.ts (academic questions); docs/plan/16 §7.3 (collaboration register). Checked 2026-10-31.

The shape of it. Researchers answer email about their work from people who have read it. The first message to a professor, a postdoc or a PhD student is about their paper, their method or their dataset, with a precise question or a concrete overlap, never a general request to "work with you" or "learn more about your lab". Undergraduates asking to join a lab are a different, well understood case: the ask is to be a research assistant, and the message shows the student read two papers from the group, took the relevant course, and has time.

The first message to a researcher. Who you are in one line (year, program, advisor if any). One sentence naming the specific paper or talk and the specific thing in it that matters to your work. One precise question (does the method hold when X, did you try Y, is the dataset available under Z) or one concrete overlap (we have data that tests your claim in a new setting). The ask: an answer by email, or twenty minutes if a call is easier. Attach nothing unless they would want it (a one page summary of your results, a link to a preprint). Eighty to a hundred and twenty words is fine here; precision beats brevity. "Dear Professor Lastname" for faculty you have not met, "Hi Firstname" for PhD students and postdocs. Sign with full name, program and institution.

Asking to join a lab. Email the professor and, if you can find them, the PhD student whose project you would join. Say which project, why it interests you (one specific thing), what you can do (a course, a tool, a prior project), and how many hours a week for how many semesters. Ask whether they take undergraduate research assistants and what they look for in one; ask for a fifteen minute meeting or office hours. Expect silence from half of them and send one polite bump after ten days. Professors read email in bursts; Tuesday to Thursday mornings during term, never the week before a deadline or in August.

The conversation. Twenty minutes, mostly about their work. Prepare three questions that show you read the paper, one about where the work is going, one about how students take part. For a collaboration, bring one concrete proposal: what you would do, what you would need from them, what each side gets, and a first small step (share a dataset, run one experiment, co-write a workshop paper). Follow up the same day with a short note that restates the step and the date.

Working together. Agree authorship and credit early and in writing (even an email): who leads, who is an author, what counts as a contribution. Share code and data with a licence and a README. Send short progress updates every two to three weeks with what worked and what did not. Cite them when you use their work, and tell them when you do.

Who to talk to for advice. Graduate students in the field (how they picked an advisor, what the lab is really like), the undergraduate research office, faculty in office hours. The question is about the path and the work, not about a spot.

What never happens: a message that could be sent to any lab, "I am passionate about research", a request to be a co-author before doing anything, a question the paper's abstract answers, a resume attached to a first email, or more than one bump. Register: precise, academic, curious, specific about the work.
