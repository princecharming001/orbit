# Asking a friend for an introduction

Sources: docs/plan/29-friends-and-autopilot.md (Part A); docs/plan/15-outreach-playbook.md §2.12; Connect The Dots and The Swarm on sharing the existence and strength of a relationship, never its content; Dalton's "The 2-Hour Job Search" on the forwardable note. Checked 2026-10-31.

A friend on Orbit pools their network with yours: you see who they know and roughly how well, they see yours, and introductions happen in one tap. That is all that crosses. A friend sees a name, a title, a company, a public LinkedIn profile, a school, whether someone is their alum, and a closeness word, knows, knows well or close, built from LinkedIn, their own labels and their own answers. Nothing from Gmail or Calendar is in it, no addresses, no notes, no messages, no scores, and nobody they hid. Each side chooses what it shares, everyone, only people at some companies, or nothing yet, and a change takes effect at once. When you wonder what a friend can see of you, the answer is the preview, which is the projection row for row.

The friend is always the connector. You never write to a friend's contact yourself, because you have no address and no standing with them; you ask the friend, and the friend forwards. This is the double opt-in the playbook teaches, with one more step built in, since the friend checks the other side before anyone is connected. A warm introduction lands several times more often than a cold note, so the extra day is worth it.

Ask a friend the way you would in person, in two parts. The first is to the friend, short and honest: who you want to meet, why them in one specific line, and an easy out, since a friend who says not this one is still a friend. The second is the note the friend can forward without editing, in the third person, two or three lines: who you are, the one thing that makes you worth fifteen minutes, the ask, and the out. Write it so the friend could paste it as it is. Never ask the friend to vouch for things they do not know about you, and never put words about the contact in the note that you did not get from the friend.

Keep the ask small. One person per ask, one ask at a time per friend, and never a list of six names. If the friend knows several people at a company, ask who they would start with and let them pick. A friend who has already forwarded one note this month is owed a thank-you before a second ask.

When the friend forwards, they put you in copy so the contact can answer you directly, and the contact's address reaches you only when they reply. Treat the first reply as the friend's gift: answer the same day, propose two windows, and keep the friend out of the scheduling thread after the first exchange so their inbox stays clean.

Reporting back is the step everyone skips and the one that keeps a friend forwarding for you. The day the friend forwards, send them one line of thanks. After the chat, tell the friend how it went in two sentences, one specific thing you learned and what you will do next, and say thank you again. If the contact never answered, tell the friend that too, without asking them to chase it; silence is normal and the friend did their part.

Return the favor without being asked. When a friend's ask arrives for one of your contacts, answer it within a day, forward when you can and say not this one when you cannot, with no reason owed. Keep the people you would rather not share marked private, so you are never deciding in the moment. A friend who sees you forward quickly and report back honestly will do the same for you, and that is the whole point of pooling.
