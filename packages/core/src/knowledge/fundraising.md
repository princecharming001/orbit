# Raising a first round

Sources: Y Combinator's guide to seed fundraising and the YC post-money SAFE; Carta's State of Pre-Seed and Q2 2026 fundraising benchmarks (median seed $4.5M at a $23.9M post-money; over nine in ten pre-seed rounds on post-money SAFEs; median caps around $10M for rounds under $1M and $15M for $1M to $2.5M); a16z and First Round on the investor process. Checked 2026-10-31. Figures move every quarter.

Rounds. Pre-seed is the first outside money: a few hundred thousand to around two million dollars from angels, pre-seed funds and accelerators, almost always on SAFEs. Seed is the first institutional round, typically two to five million dollars led by a seed fund, on SAFEs or as a priced round with a lead who sets the terms. Series A is five to twenty million dollars from a venture firm on a priced round once there is real traction. [CHANGES YEARLY: round sizes and caps drift; quote a current benchmark from the web, never a number from memory.] A SAFE (simple agreement for future equity) converts into shares at the next priced round at a valuation cap, sometimes with a discount; the post-money SAFE is the standard. A priced round sells preferred shares at an agreed valuation with a term sheet, a lead investor and legal costs. Pro rata is the right to keep a percentage in later rounds. Founders usually give up fifteen to twenty five percent across pre-seed and seed together.

How a round works. A lead sets terms, takes the largest share and signals to everyone else; angels and smaller funds fill the rest. Many seed rounds are a stack of SAFEs with no formal lead. Raise in a tight window: line up conversations, take first meetings in the same two or three weeks so investors move in parallel and feel time pressure, and never say who else is in until it is signed. Ask for the amount that buys eighteen to twenty four months of runway to the milestone the next round needs, and know that number cold.

Who to talk to and how. Warm intros beat cold notes by an order of magnitude, and the best warm intro is from a founder the investor backed. Portfolio founders, other founders, angels, former colleagues and alumni investors are the routes, in that order. A cold note can work with a seed fund that says it takes them: two lines on what the company does and why now, one line of traction, a clear ask ("would you be open to a look"), and the deck or a one pager attached or linked. The forwardable blurb is the unit of a fundraise: two or three sentences a connector can send as is. Investors are checked before they are pitched: stage, cheque size, sectors, recent deals, whether they lead. Pitching a Series B fund on a pre-seed wastes both sides' time.

The call. A first call is usually thirty minutes with a partner at a small fund, or an associate or principal who screens at a larger one. Associates and analysts do not decide; they champion. Decisions happen at a partner meeting, often weekly. The investor wants the problem, why now, the insight, traction, the team, the market, and the round: how much, on what terms, who is leading, how much is committed. Answer questions straight, say what you do not know, and ask about their process and timing at the end. Afterwards, send a short note the same day with the deck and any data room link, then keep every investor who passed or paused on a monthly update: metrics, wins, asks. Many seed cheques come from an update six months after a no.

What never goes in the first message: a long pitch, the full deck pasted in, "disruptive", a valuation, a deadline that is not real, and anything about the student's recruiting. The register is crisp, numbers first, confident, short.
